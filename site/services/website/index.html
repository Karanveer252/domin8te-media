01 · Phone first

Built for the person outside your door.

Good restaurant website design starts with one question: who is actually looking at it? Mostly it is not someone at a desk. It is a couple standing on the sidewalk deciding between you and the place across the street. It is someone on the couch at six, hungry, scrolling "dinner near me" with one thumb. It is a regular checking if you are open on a holiday Monday.

Every one of them has a phone, a short temper and a plan B. They do not want your story first, or a slideshow, or a welcome video. They want to know what you serve, whether you are open, how to get there and whether they can get a table. If the site makes them work for any of that, they close the tab and walk on.

02 · Menu up top

The menu, the hours, the way in. Before anything else.

On the sites we build, the first screen on a phone answers the four questions every guest has. What is on the menu. When you are open, today, not a table of seven days to squint at. Where you are, with a tap that opens directions. And how to book, or call, or order, whichever one you actually use.

Everything else still has a place: the room, the people, the story of the sourdough starter. It just sits below the things that get someone through the door. A good menu, written clearly and laid out well, is the best photo of your food you have.

The menu itself is real text on the page. Dish names, short descriptions, prices if you show them, sections that match how you serve: breakfast, lunch, the weekend brunch, the specials board. It reads well on a small screen and it is easy for us to change.

03 · Quick on weak signal

Quick to open on two bars of signal.

The person deciding where to eat is rarely on fast home wifi. They are on a patchy connection in a parking lot, on a train, or on a busy street where everyone's phone is fighting for the same tower. A heavy site, full of huge photos, autoplay video and extra scripts, sits there loading while they lose interest.

We keep the pages light. Images are sized for phones, the menu and hours show up first, and nothing on the page is there just for show. A quick site also helps on Google, because a page that opens fast and is easy to use on a phone is the kind of page search engines prefer to send people to.

04 · No PDF menus

Why we never use a PDF menu.

The PDF menu is the most common problem we see on restaurant sites, and it costs more than it looks. On a phone, a PDF means a download, a tiny page, and pinching and dragging to read a dish name. Plenty of people give up before they find the mains.

It hurts you on Google too. A PDF is harder for search engines to read and connect to your site than a normal page, so the dishes people are actually searching for, the ramen, the gluten free brunch, the birthday cakes, may never be tied to your name. And when the menu changes, the old PDF tends to linger in search results long after the dish is gone.

We write about it in more depth in why PDF menus hurt restaurant SEO. The short version: your menu should be a page, not a file.

05 · Kept in step

When the menu changes, everything changes with it.

A restaurant website is never finished. The soup comes back for winter. The patio closes in October. You start opening Sundays. Each of those changes has to land in more than one place, and the usual problem is that the website gets updated and the Google listing does not, or the other way around. A guest sees one set of hours on Google, another on your site, and trusts neither.

We keep them together. When a dish changes, we change the site and your Google Business Profile with it. When your hours change, you confirm them once and Google, Apple Maps and your website follow. That listing work is part of our restaurant SEO and Google service, and it is a big reason the website does its job: people who find you in search land on a site that agrees with what they just read.

06 · How we work

How we work on your restaurant website.

We do not start with a template. We start with your current site, on a phone, the way a hungry stranger would see it.

  1. Open it like a strangerWe load your site on a phone, on an ordinary connection, and try to find the menu, today's hours, directions and a way to book. Wherever that takes too long, we write it down.
  2. Fix it or rebuild itIf the bones are fine, we fix what is in the way. If the site fights every change, we rebuild it around what people come for, with the menu as a real page and the essentials up top.

Questions owners ask

Do I need a new restaurant website, or can you fix the one I have?

Often the one you have can be fixed. We open it on a phone the way a guest would, and if the structure is sound we move the menu, hours, directions and booking up top, replace any PDF menu and make it quicker. If the site fights every change, we rebuild it, and we tell you which it is in your plan.

Can I keep my domain name?

Yes. Your domain is your address, and guests, Google and your printed menus already point to it. We build the new or fixed site on the domain you already own, so nothing you have printed or shared goes stale.

What about online ordering or table booking?

We put booking or ordering where people will find it, right at the top of the site on a phone, using the service you already use. If you take bookings by phone, the button calls you. We do not push you to switch systems; we make the one you have easy to reach.

Who updates the menu when it changes?

We do. Tell us the change, whether it is a new special, a price, a seasonal dish or new hours, and we update the website and your Google Business Profile together so they always match.

How fast do I hear back after I send the form?

Within one business day. You get a plain English plan for your restaurant or café: what needs fixing first on your website and online presence, and why. It is not a pitch deck.
