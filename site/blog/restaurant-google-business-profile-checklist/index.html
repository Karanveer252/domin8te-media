A Google Business Profile checklist for restaurants

Most people meet your restaurant on Google before they meet you. This is the list we work through on every listing, written so you can do it yourself on a slow afternoon.

01 · Own it first

Before anything else, make sure it is yours.

Your restaurant Google Business Profile is the box that shows up when someone types "brunch near me" or your name into Google or Maps. For a lot of people it is the first thing they see of you. None of the rest of this list matters if you cannot get into it, so start here.

  1. Search for your place and look for the profileSearch your restaurant's name on Google while signed in. If Google asks whether you own this business, it is not claimed by you yet.
  2. Claim and verify itGoogle asks you to prove the business is yours, often by video, phone, text or a code. It can take a while, so do it before anything is urgent.
  3. Put the business in chargeThe owner of the profile should be an account the business controls, not the agency you used years ago or the nephew who set it up. People leave. The listing should not leave with them.
  4. Add other people as managers, not ownersStaff or anyone helping you can be added by email with their own login. Nobody needs your password.
02 · Name and category

Your name as it reads on the sign.

It is tempting to call the listing "Rosa's Best Pizza Downtown Delivery". Do not. Google's guidelines ask for your real name, the one on your sign, your menu and your receipts. Stuffing extra words in can get the listing suspended.

  • Name: exactly as on the signNo neighbourhoods, no dishes, no taglines. If the sign says "Rosa's", the listing says "Rosa's".
  • Primary category: the most specific true one"Restaurant" is vague. "Vietnamese restaurant", "Bakery" or "Coffee shop" tells Google and the hungry person what you are.
  • Extra categories: only what you really doA café that serves a proper brunch might add "Brunch restaurant". A bakery with seating might add "Café".
  • Description: plain words, no keyword listsA few sentences on what you serve, the way you would describe it to a neighbour.
03 · Hours, every kind

Hours people can actually trust.

Wrong hours are the fastest way to lose a regular. Someone walks over on a Monday because Google said you were open, finds the door locked, and remembers.

  1. Regular hours for every dayCheck each day, including the late night on Friday and the early close on Sunday.
  2. Holiday hours, before the holidaySet them ahead of time for statutory holidays and long weekends. Google often nudges you. Answer the nudge.
  3. Special hours for one-off changesA private event, a staff party, a snow day. Add that date and its hours only.
  4. Temporarily closed, not permanentlyClosing for a renovation? Use the temporary option. Permanently closed is hard to undo.
04 · Menu and links

The menu, the phone and the way to book.

People looking at your listing want two things: what you serve and how to get it.

  • Menu link to a real web pagePoint the menu link at a page on your site, not a PDF. A PDF is slow to open on two bars of signal, awkward to read on a phone, and harder for search engines to make sense of. We wrote more on why PDF menus hurt restaurant SEO.
  • Menu items in the profile tooGoogle lets food businesses add dishes, with descriptions and prices, straight into the profile. Keep them in step with the menu.
  • A phone number that gets answeredThe number on the listing should ring at the restaurant, not at a closed office or an old cell.
  • Website link to your own siteLink your own site, and make sure it opens fast on a phone. That is most of what our restaurant website work is about.
  • Booking and ordering linksIf you take reservations or online orders, add those links so people can act without hunting.
  • Attributes, only if they are trueOutdoor seating, vegan options, wheelchair access, takeout, good for kids. Tick only what is true every day. A diner who finds no patio will say so in a review.
05 · Photos that help

Photos that look like the place today.

People use your photos to answer simple questions. Is it nice inside? Which door is it? A clean phone lens and daylight go a long way.

  • The storefront from the streetShot from across the road, the way someone walking up would see it. This is how people recognise you on a busy block.
  • The inside, from where a guest sitsThe room at a normal moment, lights on, tables set.
  • The dishes people actually orderYour best sellers and the thing everyone comes back for, not the one plate you made once for a photo.
  • Kept currentWhen the menu changes for the season, when you repaint, when the soup comes back for winter, add new photos. Remove what is gone.
06 · Reviews, answered

Answer every review, like a person.

Reviews are read by the person deciding where to eat tonight, not just the person who wrote them. Your reply is for both of them.

  1. Reply to every one, good or badA short thank you for the good ones. If they named a dish, mention it.
  2. Keep it short and humanTwo or three sentences. Not the same copied paragraph pasted under every review.
  3. Never argue in publicFor a bad review, thank them, say sorry for the part that went wrong, and offer to talk directly.
  4. Ask, but ask fairlyGoogle does not allow offering anything in exchange for a review: no free dessert, no discount, no draw. It also does not want you asking only the happy tables. Ask everyone, or no one.
  5. Mind the anti-spam law when you message peopleIn Canada, the anti-spam law (CASL) covers commercial emails and texts. If you email or text customers to ask for a review, make sure you have their consent, say who you are, and give an easy way to opt out. A small card with the bill or a sign by the till avoids the question entirely.
07 · Posts and upkeep

Keep it moving, and keep it matching.

A listing is not a form you fill in once. The ones that do well look looked after.

  • Post your specials and eventsProfile updates are made for exactly this: the Tuesday special, a wine night, the new patio menu, the holiday hours. A photo and a couple of lines is plenty.
  • Match your website, word for wordName, address, phone and hours should read the same on your listing and your site. When they disagree, everyone starts guessing.
  • Do not forget Apple MapsPlenty of iPhone users search in Apple Maps first, and Apple has its own tool for businesses. Check your hours and details there too, and change both at the same time.
  • Check it every weekLook for new reviews, new questions and any edits suggested by the public. Anyone can suggest a change, and some go through.

Questions owners ask

Is a Google Business Profile free?

Yes. Creating, claiming and editing a Google Business Profile costs nothing. If someone calls saying you must pay to keep your restaurant listed on Google or to verify it, be careful: that is not how Google works.

How do I give someone access without sharing my password?

Add them as a user from inside your profile, using their email address, and give them the manager role. They sign in with their own Google account. You can remove them any time.

What if someone else already claimed my restaurant's listing?

First ask whoever set it up, such as a former agency, staff member or relative, to make you an owner. If you cannot reach them, Google offers a way to request access when you try to claim the profile. The current owner is asked to respond, and if they do not, Google may let you verify and take it over.

How often should I check my Google Business Profile?

Once a week is a good habit for most restaurants and cafés. Look for new reviews to answer, questions from customers, and edits other people have suggested.
