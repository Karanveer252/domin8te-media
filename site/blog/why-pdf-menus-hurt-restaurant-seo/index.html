PDF menus hide your food from Google

A PDF menu looks tidy on a desktop. On a phone outside your door, and in Google's search results, it quietly works against you. Here is why, and what to put there instead.

01 · The guest's side

Picture the person outside your door.

Someone is standing on the pavement with a phone, deciding between your place and the one across the street. They tap "Menu" on your restaurant website. Then they wait. A PDF has to download in full before it shows anything, and on two bars of signal that wait feels long. Some phones open it in another app, so the guest is pulled away from your site and has to find their way back.

When it finally opens, the menu was designed for a printed page, not a phone screen. The dish names are small, the prices are smaller, and the guest has to pinch, zoom and drag sideways to read one column at a time. Anyone hungry and in a hurry will give up long before they reach dessert.

Then there is the quiet one: an old PDF still sitting on the site, showing last season's menu. The guest reads about the summer salad, walks in for it, and finds the soup is back for winter instead. That is a small letdown at the door, and it was avoidable.

02 · What Google reads

Google reads pages better than it reads files.

Restaurant SEO starts with a simple idea: Google can only show your food to people if it can read what you serve. Google and the other search sites are built to read web pages. They can read some PDFs too, but a designed menu file is a much less reliable thing to read than a plain page of text.

A lot depends on how the PDF was made. If the menu was exported as a picture, or scanned from a printed copy, the dish names are part of an image. To a person they look like words. To Google they may be nothing at all. Your braised short rib, your oat milk flat white, your gluten free pancakes: none of it may ever be read.

When the menu is real text on a real page, it can match the things locals actually type. Searches like "ramen near me", "gluten free brunch" or "vegan pastries open now" are looking for places that serve those things. A menu page that says so in plain words gives Google a reason to show you. A menu locked inside a picture gives it nothing to go on.

03 · The wrong landing

When the PDF ranks, the guest gets stuck.

Sometimes Google does read the PDF and shows it in search results. That sounds like good news, but look at where the guest lands. They tap the result and arrive at a bare file. There is no "Book a table" button, no phone number to tap, no map, no opening hours and no menu bar leading back to the rest of your site.

A guest who lands on a proper menu page can read the dishes and book in the same breath. A guest who lands on a PDF has to read it, close it, search again and hope they find your website the second time. Every extra step is a chance for them to pick somewhere else.

04 · Keeping it current

A PDF goes stale the day you change a dish.

Menus change. A dish sells out for the season, a price moves, a new special earns its place. With a PDF, every one of those changes means going back to whoever designed it, waiting for a new file, exporting it again and uploading it over the old one. That is a lot of fuss for a price change, so it tends not to happen. The PDF drifts further from the real menu with every week.

A menu page made of text can be changed in minutes. Swap a dish name, fix a price, add a note that the soup is back. It also makes it easy to keep the menu on your Google listing in step with the menu on your site, so a guest sees the same thing wherever they look. 05 · What to do instead

Build the menu as a real page.

The fix is not complicated. Put the menu on your website as an ordinary page, written in text, laid out for a phone first. Here is what a good one holds:

  1. Clear sectionsBrunch, mains, sides, desserts, drinks: the same groups you use on the printed menu, each with its own heading so a guest can jump straight to what they want.
  2. Dish names as textWritten out, not baked into a picture, so guests and Google can both read every one.
  3. Short descriptionsA line about what is on the plate. Plain words, the way you would describe it to a regular.
  4. Prices, if you show themBig enough to read without zooming. If you prefer to leave prices off, that is your call, but be consistent.
  5. Dietary marksVegetarian, vegan, gluten free and so on, with a short key. These words are often exactly what people search for.
  6. Book, order, find usClear links to book a table, order, call or get directions, close to the menu so the guest never has to hunt.

This is how we build every restaurant website: menu, hours, directions and booking up top, quick to load on two bars of signal, and no PDF menus.

06 · Keeping the print

Keep the PDF for the printer.

None of this means throwing away a menu you love. If you have a beautifully designed printed menu, keep it. You can even offer the PDF as a download from the menu page, labelled clearly as the printable version, for anyone who wants it. Just do not make it the menu itself. The page comes first, and the file is an extra.

Questions owners ask

Can I keep my printed menu design?

Yes. Keep the printed menu for the tables and offer the PDF as an extra download for anyone who wants a printable copy. The menu people see on your website should still be a real page of text, built for a phone, so guests can read it easily and Google can read every dish.

What about photos of my menu instead of a PDF?

A photo of a menu has the same problem as a scanned PDF. The words are part of a picture, so a guest has to zoom in to read them and Google may not read them at all. Food photos are great on a menu page, but the dish names, descriptions and prices should be written as text.

Does Google read PDF menus at all?

Google can index some PDFs, especially ones with real text inside. But text inside images, or inside a scanned menu, often is not read. Even when a PDF does show up in search, it is a poor landing page: no booking button, no directions and no easy way back to the rest of your restaurant website.

How often should my menu page change?

Whenever your menu does. If a dish comes off, a price moves or a seasonal special returns, update the menu page and your Google listing at the same time. A text menu page takes minutes to change, so there is no reason for it to fall behind the food you are actually serving.
