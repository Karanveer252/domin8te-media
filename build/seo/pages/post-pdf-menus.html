Why is a PDF menu bad on a phone?

A PDF menu is slow to open and hard to read on a phone, so a hungry guest standing outside a restaurant often gives up before finding a dish. A PDF is designed for a printed page, not a small screen on two bars of signal.

Someone is on the pavement, deciding between your place and the one across the street. They tap "Menu" on your restaurant website and wait, because a PDF has to download in full before it shows anything. Some phones open it in another app, pulling the guest away from your site.

When it finally opens, the dish names are small, the prices are smaller, and the guest has to pinch, zoom and drag sideways to read one column at a time. Anyone in a hurry gives up long before dessert.

Then there is the quiet one: an old PDF still sitting on the site, showing last season's menu. The guest reads about the summer salad, walks in for it, and finds the soup is back for winter instead. That is a small letdown at the door, and it was avoidable.

Can Google read a PDF menu?

Google can read some PDF menus, but far less reliably than a plain web page of text, and a menu saved as a picture or a scan may not be read at all. Restaurant SEO starts with a simple idea: Google can only show your food to people if it can read what you serve.

A lot depends on how the PDF was made. If the menu was exported as a picture, or scanned from a printed copy, the dish names are part of an image. To a person they look like words. To Google they may be nothing at all. Your braised short rib, your oat milk flat white, your gluten free pancakes: none of it may ever be read.

When the menu is real text on a real page, it can match the things locals actually type. Searches like "ramen near me", "gluten free brunch" or "vegan pastries open now" are looking for places that serve those things. A menu page that says so in plain words gives Google a reason to show you, which is the heart of our restaurant SEO work.

When the PDF ranks, the guest gets stuck.

When Google shows a PDF menu in search results, the guest lands on a bare file with no booking button, no phone number to tap, no map, no opening hours and no way back to the rest of the restaurant website.

A guest who lands on a proper menu page can read the dishes and book in the same breath. A guest who lands on a PDF has to read it, close it, search again and hope they find your website the second time. Every extra step is a chance for them to pick somewhere else.

A PDF goes stale the day you change a dish.

A PDF menu falls out of date every time a dish, a price or a special changes, because each fix needs a new file. A restaurant menu page made of text can be changed in minutes.

With a PDF, every change means going back to whoever designed it, waiting for a new file, exporting it again and uploading it over the old one. That is a lot of fuss for a price change, so it tends not to happen, and the PDF drifts further from the real menu every week.

On a text page you swap a dish name, fix a price or add a note that the soup is back. It also makes it easy to keep the menu on your Google listing in step with the menu on your site, so a guest sees the same thing wherever they look. What should replace a PDF menu?

Replace a PDF menu with an ordinary page on your restaurant website, written in text and laid out for a phone first. Here is what a good menu page holds:

  1. Clear sectionsBrunch, mains, sides, desserts, drinks: the same groups you use on the printed menu, each with its own heading so a guest can jump straight to what they want.
  2. Dish names as textWritten out, not baked into a picture, so guests and Google can both read every one.
  3. Short descriptionsA line about what is on the plate. Plain words, the way you would describe it to a regular.
  4. Prices, if you show themBig enough to read without zooming. If you prefer to leave prices off, that is your call, but be consistent.
  5. Dietary marksVegetarian, vegan, gluten free and so on, with a short key. These words are often exactly what people search for.
  6. Book, order, find usClear links to book a table, order, call or get directions, close to the menu so the guest never has to hunt.

This is how we build every restaurant website: menu, hours, directions and booking up top, quick to load on two bars of signal, and no PDF menus.

Keep the PDF for the printer.

Keep a printed PDF menu for the tables and offer it as a clearly labelled download, but never make the PDF the menu on your website. The menu page comes first, and the file is an extra.

None of this means throwing away a menu you love. If you have a beautifully designed printed menu, keep it, and link the printable version from the menu page for anyone who wants it.

Can I keep my printed menu design?

Yes, you can keep your printed menu design for the tables and offer the PDF as an extra download for anyone who wants a printable copy. The menu people see on your website should still be a real page of text, built for a phone, so guests can read it easily and Google can read every dish.

What about photos of my menu instead of a PDF?

A photo of a menu is no better than a scanned PDF, because the words are part of a picture. A guest has to zoom in to read them and Google may not read them at all. Food photos are great on a menu page, but the dish names, descriptions and prices should be written as text.

Does Google read PDF menus at all?

Google reads some PDF menus, mainly ones with real text inside, but often misses text inside images or scanned menus. Even when a PDF does show up in search, it is a poor landing page: no booking button, no directions and no easy way back to the rest of your restaurant website.

How often should my menu page change?

Change your menu page whenever your menu changes. If a dish comes off, a price moves or a seasonal special returns, update the menu page and your Google listing at the same time. A text menu page takes minutes to change, so there is no reason for it to fall behind the food you are actually serving.
