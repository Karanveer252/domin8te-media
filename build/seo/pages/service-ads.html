Why most restaurant ads get scrolled past.

Picture someone on the couch at six, thumb moving fast, wondering about dinner. Most restaurant ads lose them in the first second. There are too many words to read. There are five offers at once: brunch, a happy hour, catering, gift cards and a new patio, all fighting for the same small square. And the photo is a stock shot of a plate that could be from anywhere, so it looks like nowhere.

People do not stop for that. They stop for something that looks like a real place near them, with one reason to go tonight or this week. That is what our restaurant ads are built around: one clear thing, shown well, that someone can decide on before their thumb moves on.

One offer, one ad.

Before we make anything, we pick the one thing each ad sells. A set lunch for the office crowd. Mussel night on Tuesdays. The quiet night you want busier. If you have three things to say, that is three ads, not one crowded one.

Keeping it to one offer does two jobs. It makes the ad easy to read in the moment someone gives it, and it makes the ad easy to judge. When an ad is about mussel night, you know exactly what it was for, and the people who tap already know what they are coming in for.

We keep the words short: the dish or the deal, the day, and where. The menu board name of the dish, not a slogan. If it would not fit on your specials board, it is probably too long for an ad.

Made for a phone and a quick decision.

Almost everyone who sees your ad will see it on a phone, mid-scroll. So we design for that screen first. Each ad is sized for the feed and for stories, so nothing gets cropped awkwardly and the offer is readable without zooming. The text sits where thumbs and buttons will not cover it.

And the ads look like your place. Your colours, your room, your plates. We use your own photos, even a quick phone shot of the special, and when there is no photo we design the ad around the dish name and your look instead of reaching for stock. Someone who walks in after seeing the ad should recognize the room.

Facebook, Instagram and Google: where restaurant ads run.

Facebook and Instagram (both run through Meta) are where you reach people nearby who were not thinking about you yet. They are scrolling, and a good picture of Tuesday's mussels gives them an idea for dinner. These ads are about being seen by the neighbourhood.

Google is different. People there are already searching: "lunch near me", "patio open tonight", the name of a dish. A Google ad puts you in front of someone who has already decided they are hungry and is choosing where to go. Plenty of places use both, and we will tell you plainly where we would start for what you want busier.

Ads work best when the rest of you is in order. Someone who sees an ad often looks you up next, so your Google listing needs the right hours and menu link (that is our restaurant SEO and Google Business Profile work), and your feed should look alive, which is what our restaurant social media service handles. The ads, the posts and the listing all tell the same story about what is on this week.

Your ad account, your ad money.

The ads run on your own ad accounts with Meta and Google, paid with your own card. You own the accounts. You set the budget, and you can see what is being spent at any time, straight from Meta or Google. We do not hold your ad money or bundle it into anything.

You give us access once at the start, the same way you would for your Google listing and social accounts. After that we make the ads, run them and keep them current. If you ever stop working with us, the accounts, the history and everything in them stay with you.

Kept current, sent somewhere good.

An ad for last month's special is worse than no ad. It sends people in asking for a dish you no longer make. So when what is on changes, we swap the ads: the soup comes back for winter, the patio closes, the set lunch gets a new main, and the ads follow. Nobody sees an ad for something you are not serving.

Where an ad sends people matters as much as the ad. A tap should land on a menu page or a booking page that loads fast on two bars of signal, not a PDF menu or a homepage that makes them hunt. If your site is not ready for that, it is something we fix through our restaurant website design work.

It depends on what you want busier. Facebook and Instagram ads reach people nearby who were not thinking about dinner yet, while Google ads reach people already searching for somewhere to eat. We look at your place and tell you plainly which we would start with, and why.

It depends, because alcohol ads come with rules from Meta, from Google and from where you are. We follow those rules when we make and run the ads, and if an offer cannot be shown the way you want, we tell you and suggest another way to say it.
